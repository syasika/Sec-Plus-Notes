# The Pretext

## Definition and Overview
- **Definition**: A setup or story an attacker uses before launching an actual attack to gain trust or create urgency/fear.
- **Components**:
  - **Actor (Attacker)**: The person executing the pretext.
  - **Story (Pretext)**: The narrative crafted by the attacker.

## Common Scam Calls Using Pretexts
### Tech Support Scam
- **Example**: "Hello sir, my name is Wendy and I'm from Microsoft Windows. This is an urgent check up call for your computer as we have found several problems with it."
  - *Analysis*: Impersonates a trusted company (Microsoft), creates urgency ("urgent check up call"), and implies a problem ("several problems").
  - *Reality*: Not Microsoft, not Wendy.

### Government Authority Scam
- **Example**: Voice mail: "This is an enforcement action executed by the US Treasury intending your serious attention."
  - *Analysis*: Impersonates a powerful authority (US Treasury), uses intimidating language ("enforcement action," "serious attention").
  - *Reality*: Unlikely to be the US Treasury; often bad grammar is a giveaway.

### Financial Incentive Scam
- **Example**: "Congratulations on your excellent payment history! You now qualify for 0% interest rates on all of your credit card accounts."
  - *Analysis*: Impersonates a credit card company, offers an attractive but unrealistic benefit ("0% interest rates").
  - *Reality*: Not a legitimate offer from a financial institution.

## Impersonation

### Definition
- **Definition**: Attackers pretend to be someone they aren't. Often called "Halloween for the fraudsters."

### Tactics Used in Impersonation
1. **Leverage Reconnaissance Details**
   - **Example**: "You can trust me, I'm with your help desk." (Impersonating an internal IT staff member).
2. **Attack as Someone Higher in Rank**
   - **Example**: "I'm the Vice President for Finance." (Exploits the victim's deference to authority).
3. **Throw Tons of Technical Details Around**
   - **Example**: "Catastrophic feedback due to the depolarization of the differential magnetometers." (Confuses the victim, making them less likely to question the legitimacy).
4. **Be a Buddy**
   - **Example**: "How about those Cubs?" (Casual conversation to establish trust).

## Eliciting Information

### Definition
- **Definition**: The process of extracting information from a victim without their realization ("Hacking the human").
- **Common with Vishing (Voice Phishing)**
  - **Easier Over Phone**: Direct interaction and less time for critical thinking compared to email.

### Psychological Techniques
1. **Create a Compelling Story (Pretext)**
   - Example: A story about a problem sending a payment that requires your banking information.

## Identity Fraud

### Definition
- **Definition**: An attacker uses your identity (personal information) to open accounts, obtain benefits, or engage in other activities under your name.
### Types of Identity Fraud
1. **Credit Card Fraud**
   - **Example**: Attacker opens a new credit card account using personal information and gains delivery address to receive the card.
2. **Bank Fraud**
   - **Example**: Attacker gains access to an existing bank account or opens a new one, potentially for money laundering.
3. **Loan Fraud**
   - **Example**: Attacker uses your name to apply for loans or leases, taking funds while you are left with debt.
4. **Government Benefits Fraud**
   - **Example**: Filing tax forms in your name and directing refunds to their own account.

## Protection Against Impersonation

1. **Never Volunteer Information**
   - Do not proactively offer personal or sensitive details; legitimate support staff should never ask for your password.
2. **Don't Disclose Personal Details**
   - Be wary of requests for your address, Social Security Number, birthday, or other identifying information over the phone, email, or other channels.
3. **Always Verify Before Revealing Information**
   - If you receive a suspicious call or message, do not trust the contact information provided by the caller/sender; call back using official, independently verified contact information.
4. **Encourage Verification as a Normal Process**
   - Organizations should foster a culture where employees are encouraged to verify callers, especially when dealing with financial or sensitive information.

## Takeaways for Exam Prep

1. **Definitions Are Key**:
   - Understand and be able to define impersonation, pretexting, eliciting information, and identity fraud.
2. **Attack Flow**:
   - **Pretext**: The setup/story.
   - **Impersonation**: The act of pretending to be someone else.
   - **Eliciting Information**: The goal of getting data.
   - **Identity Fraud**: The ultimate malicious use of that data.
3. **Impersonation Tactics**:
   - Using reconnaissance data to appear credible.
   - Claiming higher authority/rank.
   - Using overwhelming technical jargon.
   - Building rapport ("being a buddy").
4. **Types of Identity Fraud**:
   - Credit card fraud (new accounts, charges on your credit).
   - Bank fraud (account access, new accounts for illegal funds).
   - Loan fraud (loans in your name, attacker takes money).
   - Government benefits fraud (tax refunds, other benefits in your name).
5. **Protection Strategies**:
   - Never volunteer information: Especially passwords or personal details.
   - Don't disclose personal details: Be suspicious of requests for SSN, birthday, address.
   - Always verify: The most important defense; call back using official, independently verified contact information.
   - Organizational culture: Verification should be a standard, encouraged practice.

6. **The Human Element**:
   - Remember that these attacks "hack the human" by exploiting trust, fear, greed, or confusion through well-documented psychological techniques. Attackers build a "story" to justify their requests.
