# 1.1.1 Information Security

## Overview
- **Definition**: Refers to the protection of data resources from unauthorized access, attack, theft, or damage.
- **Vulnerabilities**: Data may be vulnerable due to how it is stored, transferred, or processed.

## CIA Triad
### Confidentiality
- Ensures that information can only be accessed by authorized individuals.
- **Example**: Encrypting sensitive data to ensure it cannot be read by unauthorized users.

### Integrity
- Guarantees that the data remains accurate and consistent over its lifecycle.
- **Example**: Using checksums or hash functions to verify the integrity of transferred files.

### Availability
- Ensures that information is accessible when needed, without any interruptions.
- **Example**: Implementing backup systems to ensure data availability in case of disasters.

## Additional Properties

### Non-repudiation
- Prevents a person from denying actions such as creating, modifying, or sending resources.
- **Example**: Using digital signatures for legal documents to ensure they cannot be denied by the signer.

## Key Concepts

- **Infosec**: Protecting data from unauthorized access, attacks, theft, and damage.
- **Security Models**: Frameworks that define how secure systems should operate. Examples include:
  - **Bell-LaPadula Model**
  - **Biba Model**
  - **Clark-Wilson Model**

## Takeaways
- Understand the three core properties of information security: Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability.
- Recognize the importance of non-repudiation in ensuring that actions cannot be denied.
- Familiarize yourself with various security models to understand different approaches to maintaining secure systems.

# Misinformation/Disinformation

## Definition
- **Factually Incorrect Information**: Disseminating information that is factually incorrect to others.
  - Key distinction: It's not just a difference of opinion; the information contains **incorrect details**.

## Goals
- Create **confusion and division** among different groups of people.

### Types and Tactics

#### Influence Campaigns:
- Campaigns (often on social media) designed to sway public opinion on political and social issues.
  
#### Nation-State Actors:
- Third-party governments or nation-states actively create dissension.
  - Objectives include: **divide, distract, and persuade** people to believe false narratives, or to distract from truths that might be damaging to the nation-state.

#### Advertising as an Option:
- Misinformation can be delivered through paid advertising.
  - Attackers can "buy a voice" for their opinion, bypassing organic content reach.

#### Enabled through Social Media:
- Social media platforms are ideal for spreading misinformation.
  - Attackers use social media tools to **create, share, like, and amplify** content, getting it in front of as many people as possible.

### The Misinformation Process

1. **Create Fake Users**: The attacker creates multiple accounts that are not associated with real individuals (e.g., bot accounts, fabricated profiles).
2. **Create Content**: The attacker generates the specific misinformation or misleading narrative.
3. **Post on Social Media**: One of the fake user accounts posts the created content on a social media platform.
4. **Amplify Message**: The attacker uses their other fake user accounts to "like," "share," and "follow" the initial post. Social media algorithms, designed to promote engaging content, recognize this activity and amplify the message to a larger audience.
5. **Real Users Share the Message**: Due to the amplified visibility, actual, legitimate users begin to see the misinformation and, believing it, share it with their networks.
6. **Mass Media Picks Up the Story**: Once the misinformation reaches a certain level of popularity and widespread sharing among real users, traditional mass media outlets may pick up on it, creating their own stories and further disseminating the initial misinformation.

# Brand Impersonation

## Definition
- A social engineering technique where attackers pretend to be a well-known, legitimate brand (e.g., Coca-Cola, McDonald's, Apple).
  - Leverages the instant recognition and trust associated with established brands.

### Method
- Attackers create **tens of thousands of impersonated websites**.
  - These sites are designed to be indexed by search engines (e.g., Google).
- When a user searches for the legitimate brand, they might be redirected to one of these impersonated sites (e.g., by clicking on a malicious ad in search results or through compromised search engine optimization).

### Consequences
- Upon visiting the impersonated site, users are often presented with deceptive pop-ups or messages (e.g., "You won! Special offer! Download the video!").
  - This usually leads to a **malware infection** on the user's computer.
- Potential outcomes of malware infection include:
  - Displaying unwanted ads (adware).
  - Site tracking (spyware).
  - Data exfiltration (stealing sensitive information from the user's computer).

# Exam Takeaways: Other Social Engineering Attacks

## Core Concepts for the Exam:

1. **Understand the Nuance of Misinformation/Disinformation**:
   - **Key Definition**: It's about *factually incorrect information*. This distinguishes it from mere differing opinions.
   - **Attacker’s Goal**: Remember that the primary objective is **confusion and division**, often leading to a specific political or social agenda.
   - **Actors**: Be aware that **nation-state actors** are significant players in this space, using these tactics for geopolitical influence, distraction, or persuasion.
   - **Channels**: Social media is a prime channel, but don't forget **advertising** as a paid method for spreading false narratives.

2. **Memorize The Misinformation Process (Crucial Diagram/Flow)**:
   - Be able to recount the six steps in order and explain what happens at each stage:
     1. **Fake Users**: Creation of non-existent personas to lend false credibility.
     2. **Create Content**: The actual false narrative is crafted.
     3. **Post on Social Media**: Initial seeding of the content.
     4. **Amplify Message**: Leveraging fake users (bots, sockpuppets) to generate initial engagement, which then triggers social media algorithms.
     5. **Real Users Share**: The spread to genuine users, often unknowingly.
     6. **Mass Media Picks Up**: The ultimate goal of broad legitimization and wider reach.
   - *Why it's effective*: Emphasize how social media algorithms, designed for engagement, inadvertently act as amplifiers for these attacks once initial fake engagement is generated.

3. **Grasp Brand Impersonation Mechanics and Impact**:
   - **Definition**: Pretending to be a *trusted, well-known company*. This leverages existing public trust.
   - **Scale**: Recognize that attackers create *tens of thousands* of these fake sites.
   - **Discovery Method**: Understand how these sites get found: through search engine indexing (Google) and users being redirected, often via malicious ads or manipulated search results.
   - **Immediate Threat**: The primary goal here is often direct **malware infection**.
   - **Malware Types & Consequences**: Be ready to list the common types of malware delivered (adware, spyware) and their effects (displaying ads, tracking user activity, **data exfiltration**).
   - **User Lures**: Pop-ups offering "prizes" or "special offers" are common tactics to entice users to download malware.

## Broader Security Context

- **Social Engineering Foundation**: Both types of attacks are fundamentally social engineering. They prey on human psychology – trust, curiosity, desire for information, or fear.
- **Critical Thinking & Verification**: While not explicitly covered as defense, understanding these attacks highlights the importance of critical thinking, verifying sources, and being wary of unsolicited offers or sensational claims, especially online.
- **Impact on Organizations**: Brand impersonation directly damages a company's reputation and can lead to customer data breaches, while misinformation can indirectly affect businesses by creating public unrest or distrust.
- **Supply Chain Implications**: Misinformation could target a company's partners or suppliers, creating vulnerabilities in the extended enterprise.
