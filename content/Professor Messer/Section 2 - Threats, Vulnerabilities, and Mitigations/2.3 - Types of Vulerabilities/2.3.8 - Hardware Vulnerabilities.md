# Hardware Vulnerabilities

## Introduction to Hardware Vulnerabilities

- **Ubiquity of Hardware Devices**: We are surrounded by hardware devices, many of which, especially those in the Internet of Things (IoT) category, do not have an accessible operating system interface. Examples include air conditioning controls, clock-in systems, smart home devices like light bulbs, garage doors, refrigerators, and door locks.
- **Security Risk**: These connected devices can pose significant security risks and serve as potential entry points for attacks.
- **Changing Landscape**: The security landscape has grown significantly due to the proliferation of IoT devices. Previously, security concerns focused on traditional operating systems such as Windows, macOS, Linux, and mobile phones. Now, every single IoT device connected to the network is a potential security concern.
- **Importance of Updates**: Keeping the firmware or software running inside these hardware devices up-to-date is crucial.

## Firmware

- **Definition**: Firmware is the low-level software that runs on hardware devices; it acts as their operating system. Users often have no direct access to this operating system.
- **Vendor Responsibility**: Manufacturers (vendors) are typically the only ones who can fix or update their hardware's firmware.
    - **Assumptions**:
        1. They are aware of the problem/vulnerability.
        2. They care enough to address it.

### Case Study: Trane Comfortlink II Thermostats

- These automated thermostats are controllable via mobile devices.
- In April 2014, Trane was notified of three vulnerabilities.
- **April 2015**: Two vulnerabilities were patched (a year later).
- **January 2016**: The third vulnerability was finally patched (almost two years after the initial notification).
- **Implication**: This extended period without patches highlights a significant security concern. Traditional OS patches are usually released within a month, but these IoT devices often lag significantly.

## End-of-Life (EOL) & End-of-Service Life (EOSL)

### End-of-Life (EOL)

- **Definition**: The manufacturer stops selling a product.
- **Support**: The manufacturer may continue supporting the product with security patches and updates for a period after EOL, serving as a warning sign that future support might cease.

### End-of-Service Life (EOSL)

- **Definition**: The manufacturer stops selling and no longer provides any support for the product.
- **Key Characteristic**: No ongoing security patches or updates will be provided. This is a significant security concern.
- **Exception**: Manufacturers may offer premium-cost support options, but this is usually prohibitively expensive for most customers.
- **Action**: If equipment reaches EOSL, it should be replaced as soon as possible to ensure continuous security updates.

## Legacy Platforms

- **Definition**: Devices or systems that have been installed for a long time and often run older operating systems, applications, or middleware. They are typically outdated and pose risks.
- **End-of-Life Software**: Legacy platforms often run software that has reached its EOL (End-of-Life) or even EOSL (End-of-Service Life).
- **Risk vs. Return**: Organizations must balance the security risks of continuing to use legacy devices/software against the business value they provide.
- **Challenges**: Replacing legacy platforms can be difficult, especially if they are critical to organizational goals and processes in large infrastructures, data centers, or remote sites.
- **Mitigation Strategies (if immediate replacement isn't feasible)**:
    - **Additional Security Protections**: Implement extra layers of security around these devices.
    - **Additional Firewall Rules**: Limit direct network access to legacy devices to restrict who can connect to them.
    - **IPS Signatures**: Deploy Intrusion Prevention System (IPS) signatures specifically designed to detect and block attacks targeting known vulnerabilities in older operating systems or applications.
- **Goal**: Create a strategic path for eventual replacement while actively managing and mitigating security risks.

## Exam Takeaways

1. **Hardware Vulnerabilities are Pervasive**: Understand that any connected hardware device, especially IoT, can be a security vulnerability, even if it doesn't have a traditional accessible OS. This expands the attack surface significantly.
2. **Firmware is the OS of Hardware**: Know that firmware controls hardware and its security depends entirely on the manufacturer. **Key Problem**: Manufacturers may be slow (or unwilling) to patch vulnerabilities as seen in the Trane thermostat example.
3. **Distinguish EOL and EOSL**:
    - **EOL (End-of-Life)**: Manufacturer stops selling but might still support with patches. It’s a warning sign.
    - **EOSL (End-of-Service Life)**: Manufacturer stops selling AND supporting, leading to no more security patches. This is a critical security risk.
    - **Action**: Replace devices at EOSL immediately.
4. **Legacy Platforms Pose Significant Risk**: Be aware that old, outdated hardware and software are inherently vulnerable due to unpatched exploits or known weaknesses.
    - **Challenge**: They often support critical business operations, making replacement difficult.
    - **Mitigation**: If immediate replacement isn’t feasible, implement compensating controls like strict firewall rules and specific IPS signatures.
5. **Security Approach Shift**: The rise of IoT requires a fundamental change in how organizations approach security, moving beyond just traditional endpoints and servers to include all networked hardware.
6. **Vendor Dependency**: Recognize that hardware security heavily relies on vendors. Their commitment (or lack thereof) to timely patching and long-term support directly impacts your security posture.
