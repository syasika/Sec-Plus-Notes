# Introduction to Digital Certificates

## Overview
- **Definition**: A digital certificate is a public key certificate that binds a public key with a digital signature and other details about the key holder. 
- **Purpose**: Primarily to add trust in IT security, allowing us to verify the identity of entities (people, servers, etc.) we might not otherwise know.

## Mechanisms for Trust
### Public Key Infrastructure (PKI)
- **Definition**: Uses Certificate Authorities (CAs) for centralized trust.
- **Example**: A browser or operating system inherently trusts certain CAs, and if a website's certificate is signed by one of these trusted CAs, the browser will trust that website.

### Web of Trust
- **Definition**: A decentralized model where multiple individuals sign each other's certificates, establishing trust through a network of known entities.
- **Example**: If Alice signs Bob’s certificate and both are known to Charlie, then Charlie can trust Bob if he trusts Alice.

## Certificate Creation
- **Built-in Options**: Can be built into the Operating System (e.g., Windows Domain Services).
- **Third-party Software**: Numerous third-party software options exist for creating certificates.

# Core Concepts & Definitions

### Digital Certificate
- A digital ID that binds a public key to an individual or organization, digitally signed by a trusted third party (CA). Essential for authentication and establishing trust in digital communications.

### X.509 Standard
- The universal standard for digital certificates.
- **Common Fields**: Serial Number, Issuer, Subject Name, Public Key, Signature Algorithm, Extensions.

### Certificate Authority (CA)
- A trusted entity that issues and manages digital certificates.
- The cornerstone of the PKI model of trust.

### Root of Trust
- An inherently trusted component (hardware, software, firmware, or a CA) from which all other trust in a system or hierarchy is derived. It's the starting point for validation.

### Certificate Signing Request (CSR)
- **Process**: An applicant generates a key pair and sends their public key and identifying information to a CA for signing.
- Crucial step for obtaining a legitimate certificate.

### Private CA
- An organization’s internal CA, used to issue certificates for internal systems. Requires all internal devices to explicitly trust this CA.

### Self-Signed Certificate
- A certificate signed by its own creator, rather than a CA.
- Only trusted if the signing entity is inherently trusted (e.g., an internal CA within an organization).

### Wildcard Certificate
- An X.509 certificate with a Subject Alternative Name (SAN) extension that uses a wildcard character (`*`) to secure multiple subdomains under a single domain (e.g., `*.example.com`).
- Simplifies management.

# Key Processes & How They Work

## Establishing Trust via CAs
- **Chain of Trust**: Your browser trusts a Root CA (pre-installed) -> the Root CA signs Intermediate CAs -> Intermediate CAs sign end-entity certificates (like a website's cert) -> your browser validates the chain back to a trusted Root CA.

## Certificate Signing Request (CSR)
- **Steps**:
  1. An applicant generates a key pair.
  2. Sends their public key and identifying information to a CA for signing.
- Emphasize the role of the public key and the CA's validation process.

# Comparisons & Contrasts

## PKI vs. Web of Trust
- **PKI**: Centralized trust model with a hierarchy of CAs.
- **Web of Trust**: Decentralized trust model relying on individual endorsements.

## Public CA vs. Private CA
- **Public CA**:
  - Trusted globally by default in browsers/OS.
  - Used for public-facing services.
- **Private CA**:
  - Built in-house, only trusted by devices explicitly configured to do so.
  - Used for internal services where public trust isn't needed or privacy is paramount.

## CRL vs. OCSP Stapling
- **CRL (Certificate Revocation List)**:
  - Browser downloads a potentially large file, checks for the certificate's serial number.
  - Inefficient due to file size and download time.
  
- **OCSP (Online Certificate Status Protocol) Stapling**:
  - Server gets the revocation status from the CA, signs it, and sends it directly with the certificate during the handshake.
  - More efficient and scalable.

# Practical Implications & Security Considerations

### Browser Security
- Your browser’s built-in list of trusted Root CAs is fundamental to secure web browsing.

### Heartbleed (Historical Context)
- A critical vulnerability that highlighted the importance of certificate revocation when private keys are compromised. It led to mass certificate revocations and reissues.

### Scalability of Revocation
- Understand why CRLs are inefficient for large-scale operations and how OCSP stapling addresses this.

### Trust in Internal Networks
- Private CAs are a common and effective way to secure internal communications without incurring costs or exposing internal details to public CAs.

### Browser Support for OCSP Stapling
- Not all browsers/apps support OCSP (or check it properly) – this is a potential weak point in the revocation chain, as some clients might still trust revoked certificates if they don't perform necessary checks.

# Exam Study Tips

## Diagrams
- Draw out the CSR process and the OCSP stapling process. Visualizing these helps immensely.

## Scenario Questions
- Think about scenarios: "An attacker compromises a web server's private key. What is the immediate action regarding the certificate, and how does the revocation system work?"

## Definitions and Acronyms
- Memorize the full names and functions of all acronyms (CA, PKI, CSR, SAN, CRL, OCSP).

## "Why" Questions
- Don't just know *what* something is; know *why* it's important or *why* a particular method is used over another. (e.g., Why use a Private CA? Why is OCSP stapling better than CRL?)
