# 1.1.1 Information Security

## Overview
- **Definition**: Refers to the protection of data resources from unauthorized access, attack, theft, or damage.
- **Vulnerabilities**: Data may be vulnerable due to how it is stored, transferred, or processed.

## CIA Triad
### Confidentiality
- Ensures that information can only be accessed by authorized individuals.
- **Example**: Encrypting sensitive data to ensure it cannot be read by unauthorized users.

### Integrity
- Guarantees that the data remains accurate and consistent over its lifecycle.
- **Example**: Using checksums or hash functions to verify the integrity of transferred files.

### Availability
- Ensures that information is accessible when needed, without any interruptions.
- **Example**: Implementing backup systems to ensure data availability in case of disasters.

## Additional Properties

### Non-repudiation
- Prevents a person from denying actions such as creating, modifying, or sending resources.
- **Example**: Using digital signatures for legal documents to ensure they cannot be denied by the signer.

## Key Concepts

- **Infosec**: Protecting data from unauthorized access, attacks, theft, and damage.
- **Security Models**: Frameworks that define how secure systems should operate. Examples include:
  - **Bell-LaPadula Model**
  - **Biba Model**
  - **Clark-Wilson Model**

## Takeaways
- Understand the three core properties of information security: Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability.
- Recognize the importance of non-repudiation in ensuring that actions cannot be denied.
- Familiarize yourself with various security models to understand different approaches to maintaining secure systems.

# Data Encryption Methods

## Encrypting Stored Data (Data at Rest)
- **Purpose**: Protect data on storage devices such as SSDs, hard drives, USB drives, and cloud storage.

### Full-Disk and Partition/Volume Encryption
- Encrypts everything on a storage device or volume.
- **Examples**:
    - **BitLocker** (Windows): Full disk encryption solution.
    - **FileVault** (macOS): Full disk encryption solution.
    - Other operating systems have similar utilities.

### File Encryption
- Encrypts individual files or folders, not the entire volume.
- **Examples**:
    - **EFS (Encrypting File System)** (Windows): File-level encryption built into the NTFS file system. To enable it, right-click the file/folder > Properties > Advanced Attributes > Check "Encrypt contents to secure data".
    - Third-party utilities are available for Windows, macOS, and Linux.

## Database Encryption
- **Purpose**: Protecting stored data within a database and the transmission of that data.

### Transparent Encryption
- Encrypts all database information with a symmetric key.
- **Mechanism**: Data is encrypted when written to the database and decrypted when read, transparently to the application.
- **Overhead**: Significant due to encryption/decryption each time data is accessed.
- **Benefit**: Simplifies management as all data is protected by default.
- **Example**: An entire database table (Employee ID, First Name, Last Name, SSN) could be encrypted into unreadable ciphertext.

### Record-Level Encryption (Column-Level)
- Encrypts individual columns or records within a database.
- **Mechanism**: Uses separate symmetric keys for specific sensitive columns.
- **Benefit**: Allows non-sensitive data to remain in plaintext, reducing encryption/decryption overhead and improving search performance on unencrypted columns.
- **Example**: Only the SSN column in an employee database is encrypted, while Employee ID, First Name, and Last Name remain in plaintext.

## Transport Encryption
- **Purpose**: Protecting data as it traverses the network (data in transit).
- Commonly used and often transparent to the user.

### Encrypting in the Application
- **Browsers**: Communicate using HTTPS (Hypertext Transfer Protocol Secure).
    - Encrypts web traffic between the browser and the server.

### VPN (Virtual Private Network)
- Encrypts all data transmitted over the network, regardless of the application.
- Creates an encrypted tunnel between two points.
- **Types**:
    - **Client-based VPN**: Used for individual users to remotely access a network. Often uses SSL/TLS.
    - **Site-to-site VPN**: Connects two networks (e.g., corporate office and remote branch). Often uses IPsec.

## Encryption Algorithms
- **Definition**: The specific "formula" or mathematical process used during encryption and decryption.
- **Requirement**: The proper algorithm must be used for both encryption and decryption to be successful.
- **Decision**: Both sides (sender and receiver) must agree on the algorithm before encrypting the data.
- **Transparency**: Details of algorithms are often hidden from the end-user.
- **Advantages/Disadvantages**: Algorithms vary in:
    - **Security level**
    - **Speed**
    - **Complexity of implementation**

### Encryption Algorithm Comparison (DES vs. AES)
- **Key takeaway for exam**: You don't need to know the specific steps of each algorithm, but understand that different algorithms are incompatible. You cannot encrypt with DES and decrypt with AES.
- **DES (Data Encryption Standard)**:
    - Older standard, 64-bit plaintext input, 16 rounds of processing.
    - Produces 64-bit ciphertext.
- **AES (Advanced Encryption Standard)**:
    - Modern standard. Takes plaintext and a secret key, applies a cipher, and produces ciphertext.
    - Supports different key lengths: 128, 192, 256 bits, which affect the output ciphertext size and security.

## Cryptographic Keys
- **Core Principle**: There's very little that isn't known about the cryptographic process itself. The algorithm is usually a known entity (often public, increasing trust as it can be scrutinized for weaknesses).
- **The only thing you don't know is the key.**
- **Analogy**: A physical door lock (algorithm) is well-understood, but without the specific key, you can't open it.
- **Key's Role**: The key determines the output:
    - Encrypted data
    - Hash value
    - Digital signature
- **Critical Security Implication**: Keep your key private! It is the only thing protecting your data. If someone gains access to your key, they gain access to your data.

## Key Lengths
- **Security**: Larger keys tend to be more secure because they make brute-force attacks significantly harder.
- **Brute-Force Attacks**: Attackers try every possible key combination.
- **Symmetric Encryption**:
    - **128-bit or larger symmetric keys are common and considered secure today**. These numbers get larger as time goes on due to advancements in computing power (Moore's Law).
- **Asymmetric Keys**: Significantly longer, often 3,072 bits or more, due to the underlying mathematical complexity (prime numbers).

## Key Stretching
- **Purpose**: To strengthen weak keys (like user-chosen passwords) against brute-force attacks.
- **Mechanism**: Involves performing multiple rounds of hashing on the original key/password.
- **Effect**: Increases the computational time required for an attacker to crack the key, even if the original key is simple.

# Takeaways for Exam

## Data States and Encryption Methods
- **Data at Rest**:
    - **Full-disk encryption**: Protects entire storage devices (e.g., BitLocker, FileVault).
    - **File encryption**: Protects individual files/folders (e.g., EFS in Windows).
- **Data in Transit**:
    - **Application-level**: HTTPS for web traffic.
    - **Network-level**: VPNs create encrypted tunnels. Client-based VPNs often use SSL/TLS; site-to-site VPNs often use IPsec.
- **Database Encryption**:
    - **Transparent encryption**: Encrypts the entire database, requiring decryption on every access (higher overhead).
    - **Record-level/Column-level encryption**: Encrypts only specific sensitive columns, reducing overhead and allowing faster access to non-sensitive data.

## Encryption Algorithms vs. Keys
- **Encryption Algorithm**: The mathematical formula. It is generally public and known.
    - Both parties must use the same algorithm for successful encryption/decryption.
    - Algorithms differ in security, speed, and complexity.
- **Cryptographic Key**: The secret input to the algorithm.
    - The key is the only piece of unknown information for an attacker.
    - Crucial: Always keep private keys private; their compromise means data compromise.

## Key Lengths and Brute-Force Attacks
- **Larger keys = more secure**.
- Longer keys make brute-force attacks (trying every possible combination) computationally infeasible within a reasonable timeframe.
- **Symmetric keys**: Commonly 128-bit or larger. These lengths evolve with computing power.
- **Asymmetric keys**: Significantly longer, often 3,072 bits or more, due to the underlying mathematical complexity (prime numbers).

## Key Stretching
- **Purpose**: To strengthen weak keys (like user-chosen passwords) against brute-force attacks.
- **Mechanism**: Involves performing multiple rounds of hashing on the original key/password.
- **Effect**: Increases the computational time required for an attacker to crack the key, even if the original key is simple.
