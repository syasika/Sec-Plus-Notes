# Introduction to PKI

## Overview
- **Definition**: A broad term in cryptography, referring to the policies, procedures, hardware, software, and people responsible for managing digital certificates.
- **Digital Certificates**: PKI handles their creation, distribution, management, storage, and revocation.
- **Complexity**: Implementing PKI in small companies is a significant endeavor requiring extensive planning and decision-making about encryption methods.
- **Binding Public Keys**: PKI also refers to the binding of public keys to people or devices through a **Certificate Authority (CA)**.
- **Core Concept**: It's all about **trust**—verifying that a user or device is genuinely who they claim to be.

## Symmetric Encryption
- **Concept**: Uses a single, shared key for both encryption and decryption. Encrypt with the key; decrypt with the same key.
- **Security**: If the key gets compromised, you need a new one. Security heavily relies on keeping this single key secret.
- **Terminology**: Also known as a **secret key algorithm** or a **shared secret**.
- **Scalability Issues**: Doesn't scale well for many users/devices and challenging to securely distribute and manage keys among numerous parties.
- **Advantages**:
    - **Very fast to use**.
    - **Less overhead compared to asymmetric encryption**.
- **Common Use**: Often combined with asymmetric encryption, where asymmetric encryption is used to securely exchange a symmetric key, then symmetric encryption is used for bulk data transfer.

## Asymmetric Encryption (Public Key Cryptography)
- **Concept**: Uses two mathematically related keys—public and private—for encryption and decryption.
- **Key Pair**:
    - **Private Key**:
        - **Keep this secret.** Only one person or device should have access.
        - **Cannot be derived from the public key.**
        - **Only key that can decrypt data encrypted with its corresponding public key.**
    - **Public Key**:
        - **Anyone can see this key.** Can be given away freely.
        - **Used by others to encrypt data that only the private key holder can decrypt.**
- **Key Generation**: Both keys are built simultaneously using a key generation program involving lots of randomization and large prime numbers, with complex mathematics behind it. This process typically happens only once for an individual or entity.
- **Encryption Process Example (Bob & Alice)**:
    1. Alice generates her public/private key pair. She distributes her **public key** to everyone, including Bob, and keeps her **private key** secret.
    2. Bob writes a plaintext message ("Hello, Alice").
    3. Bob uses Alice's **public key** to encrypt the plaintext, creating ciphertext.
    4. Bob sends the ciphertext to Alice.
    5. Alice receives the ciphertext. She uses her **private key** to decrypt it, revealing the original plaintext message.
    6. Crucially, even if someone intercepts the ciphertext and has Alice's public key, they cannot decrypt the message because they do not have Alice's private key.

## Key Escrow
- **Concept**: A practice where someone else holds your decryption keys (private keys).
    - Your private keys are held by a third-party entity or an internal department within your organization.
- **Legitimate Business Arrangement**:
    - **Business Continuity**: If an employee leaves, the business might need access to their encrypted information. Key escrow ensures this data remains accessible.
    - **Government Agencies**: May need to decrypt partner data for legal or operational reasons.
- **Controversy**: Handing over private keys to a third party can be controversial due to privacy and security concerns. However, it may still be required in some scenarios to maintain uptime, availability, or comply with regulations.

# Takeaways for Exam

1. **Understand the overarching purpose of PKI**:
    - It's a comprehensive system (policies, tech, people) for managing digital certificates and establishing trust in a cryptographic environment by binding public keys to identities via Certificate Authorities (CAs).

2. **Differentiate Symmetric vs. Asymmetric Encryption**:
    - **Symmetric:**
        - **Key**: Single, shared key.
        - **Process**: Encrypt and decrypt with the same key.
        - **Speed**: Fast, low overhead.
        - **Scalability**: Poor; difficult to securely distribute and manage keys for many parties.
        - **Analogy**: A single key for a briefcase.
    - **Asymmetric (Public Key Cryptography):**
        - **Key**: Two mathematically related keys (public and private).
        - **Process**: Encrypt with the recipient's public key, decrypt with the recipient's private key.
        - **Speed**: Slower, higher overhead than symmetric.
        - **Scalability**: Excellent for key distribution; public keys can be freely shared.
        - **Security**: Private key cannot be derived from the public key. Only the private key can decrypt data encrypted with its corresponding public key.
        - **Analogy**: A mailbox (public key) where anyone can drop a letter (encrypted message), but only the owner (private key holder) can open it.

3. **Know the roles of Public and Private Keys**:
    - **Public Key**: Freely distributed, used by anyone to encrypt data for the owner of the key pair.
    - **Private Key**: Kept secret by the owner, used to decrypt data encrypted with its corresponding public key.
    - **Crucial Point**: You cannot derive the private key from the public key, ensuring the security of asymmetric encryption.

4. **Understand Key Generation Process**:
    - Involves complex mathematical algorithms using large random and prime numbers to create mathematically linked public and private keys simultaneously.

5. **Recognize the Key Escrow Practice**:
    - A third party holds copies of private decryption keys.
    - Used for business continuity, legal/governmental access.
    - Raises significant privacy and security concerns but is sometimes required for operational resilience.

6. **Combined Use**: Remember that symmetric and asymmetric encryption are often used together. Asymmetric encryption is great for securely exchanging a symmetric key, which then encrypts the actual bulk data due to its speed.
