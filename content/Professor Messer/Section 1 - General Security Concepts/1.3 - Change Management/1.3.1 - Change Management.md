# 1.2 Change Management

## Overview
- **Definition**: A formal process for managing changes within an organization's IT environment to ensure proper functioning, minimize risk, and maintain system stability and security.
- **Importance**: Crucial in large organizations where a single change can affect hundreds or thousands of systems.

### How to Make a Change
- Examples of common changes include:
  - Upgrading software
  - Patching applications
  - Configuring firewalls
  - Modifying switch ports
- In corporate environments, a single change can impact numerous systems.
- **Formal Process**: Required to ensure proper functioning.

### Common Risks in the Enterprise
- Frequent changes (e.g., monthly OS updates, constant application updates) pose significant risks.
- Systems that are not updated are often less secure.
- **Importance**: Staying on top of changes is critical for security.

### Often Overlooked or Ignored
- Lack of formal change management can lead to:
  - Applications not working properly.
  - Inconsistencies in how applications use the operating system.
- Formal processes help prevent these issues.

## Key Components of Change Management

### Clear Policies
- **Frequency** and duration of changes.
- **Installation process steps**.
- **Rollback procedures** for troubleshooting.

### Challenges
- Implementing formal change control can be difficult if an organization lacks existing processes or has a culture resistant to such practices.

## Change Approval Process

### Purpose of a Formal Process
- **Avoid Downtime**: Maintain system availability.
- **Avoid Confusion**: Ensure everyone is informed about the changes.
- **Avoid Mistakes**: Reduce errors during implementation.

### Typical Approval Process
1.  **Complete the Request Forms**: Standardized forms collect necessary information for the central committee.
2.  **Determine the Purpose of the Change**: Clearly state why the change is needed.
3.  **Identify the Scope of the Change**: Determine which systems and users will be affected (from one individual to an entire company).
4.  **Schedule a Date and Time of the Change**: Plan when the change will occur.
5.  **Determine Affected Systems and Impact**: Analyze direct and indirect impacts on systems and potential consequences.
6.  **Analyze Risk Associated with the Change**:
    - Assess risks of making the change (e.g., breaking things, data corruption).
    - Assess risks of not making the change (e.g., security vulnerabilities, application unavailability, unexpected downtime).
7.  **Get Approval from the Change Control Board (CCB)**: The central committee reviews and approves or denies the request.
8.  **End-User Acceptance After Completion**: Users confirm that the updated system/application works as expected without issues.

## Ownership

### Who Owns the Process?
- An individual or entity (e.g., department) that needs to make a change owns the process.
- They typically do not perform the technical changes (e.g., IT team handles software upgrades).
- The owner manages the process:
    - Receives updates.
    - Ensures the process is followed and acceptable.

### Example
- **Shipping and Receiving Department**: Owns the process for upgrading address label printers. The IT team performs the actual upgrade.

---

## Stakeholders

### Who is Impacted by a Change?
- **Direct Stakeholders**: Individuals or departments directly affected (e.g., shipping/receiving).
- **Indirect Stakeholders**: Departments that might be indirectly impacted (e.g., accounting, product delivery times).

### Example: Upgrade Software for Shipping Labels
- **Direct Stakeholders**:
    - Shipping and receiving department.
- **Indirect Stakeholders**:
    - Accounting (for reports based on shipping data).
    - Product delivery timeframes (if shipping is delayed).
    - Revenue recognition/CEO visibility (if product delivery affects financial reporting).

---

## Impact Analysis

### Determine Risk Value
- Assign a risk value to the change (e.g., high, medium, low).

### Risks of Making or Not Making Changes
- **Risks of Making the Change**:
    - The "fix" doesn't work.
    - The "fix" breaks something else.
    - Operating system failures.
    - Data corruption (requires backups).
- **Risks of Not Making the Change**:
    - Security vulnerabilities (leaving systems exposed).
    - Application unavailability.
    - Unexpected downtime to other services.

### Balancing Risks
- The Change Control Board must balance these risks to make informed decisions.

## Test Results

### Sandbox Testing Environment
- A safe space where changes can be tested extensively without affecting live systems.
- Purpose: To test and confirm functionality before deploying in a production environment.

### Use Before Deploying to Production
- Try the upgrade, apply the patch, or make the change.
- Test thoroughly and confirm functionality before deployment.
- Can load a duplicate environment identical to production for testing.

### Confirm Backout Plan
- Test the ability to move everything back to the original configuration if needed.
- A sandbox can't consider every possibility, so thorough testing of the backout plan is crucial.

---

## Backout Plan

### Why You Need One
- **Assumption**: "The change will work perfectly" is often incorrect.
- Always have a way to revert your changes.
- **Principle**: Prepare for the worst, hope for the best.
- **Challenge**: Some changes are hard to revert (e.g., database schema changes).
- **Critical Requirement**: Good backups are essential. If both the change and backout plan fail, good backups are your last resort.

---

## Maintenance Window

### When is the Change Happening?
- **Challenging Decision**: Scheduling changes can be difficult.
- **During Workday**: May not be ideal due to potential downtime affecting production.
- **Overnights**: Often better for minimizing user impact. Challenging for 24/7 operations.
- **Time of Year**: Consideration (e.g., retail networks often "frozen" during holiday seasons).

---

## Standard Operating Procedure (SOP)

### Change Management is Critical
- Affects everyone in the organization.
- The process must be well-documented and available on the Intranet.
- No one should make changes without formal approval.

### Updating SOPs
- The SOP is a "living document" that evolves over time to improve efficiency and fit changing company requirements.

# Key Takeaways for Exam Preparation

1.  **Definition and Importance**: Change management is a structured approach to managing IT system changes, essential for large organizations due to the scale and frequency of changes. Its primary goals are minimizing disruption, maintaining security, and ensuring operational stability.
2.  **Risk Mitigation**: Changes introduce risks. A formal process helps identify, analyze, and mitigate these risks. Consider both the risks of making a change and the risks of not making one (e.g., security vulnerabilities).
3.  **Formal Process Steps**:
    - **Request**: Document the change (forms, purpose, scope).
    - **Assess**: Determine affected systems, impact, and associated risks.
    - **Schedule**: Plan the timing (maintenance window).
    - **Approve**: Get sign-off from the Change Control Board (CCB).
    - **Implement**: Execute the change (often by IT, not the owner).
    - **Verify**: User acceptance testing.
    - **Document**: Update SOPs, record results.
4.  **Ownership vs. Execution**: The application/data owner initiates and manages the process, while IT typically executes technical changes.
5.  **Stakeholder Identification**: Changes can have far-reaching impacts across different departments (e.g., a shipping label software upgrade affecting accounting and CEO visibility).
6.  **Testing is Non-Negotiable**: Use a sandbox testing environment to thoroughly test changes and backout plans.
7.  **The Backout Plan is Essential**: Always have a documented procedure to revert a change if something goes wrong.
8.  **Backups are Paramount**: Good backups ensure you can recover from failures in the change process.
9.  **Maintenance Window Strategy**: Careful scheduling minimizes impact on users and production (e.g., off-hours, weekends).
10. **Documentation (SOPs)**: All processes must be well-documented, accessible, and regularly updated to ensure consistency and accountability.
