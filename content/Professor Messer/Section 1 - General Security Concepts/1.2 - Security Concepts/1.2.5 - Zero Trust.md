# Introduction to Zero Trust

## Traditional Network Security Model (Pre-Zero Trust)
- **Overview**: In traditional networks, security measures are often relaxed on the inside once the initial firewall is passed. This means that both authorized and unauthorized individuals or malicious software can move freely within the network.
- **Example Analogy**: Think of a strong outer wall (firewall), but an open interior if someone breaches it.

## What is Zero Trust?
- **Definition**: Zero Trust is a holistic approach to network security that covers every device, process, and person. It fundamentally shifts the principle from "trust but verify" to "never trust, always verify."
- **Core Principle**: Every access attempt must be verified, making no entity inherently trusted.
- **Security Checks/Controls**: Examples include multi-factor authentication (MFA), encryption, granular permissions, additional firewalls, and monitoring.

## Planes of Operation

### Data Plane
- **Function**: Processes actual network data such as frames, packets, and data flows.
- **Actions**: Includes tasks like processing, forwarding, encrypting, and Network Address Translation (NAT).
- **Example Analogy**: Similar to the physical movement of an airplane from one point to another, with ports on a switch/router/firewall handling traffic.

### Control Plane
- **Function**: Manages actions in the data plane by defining policies and rules.
- **Actions**: Involves tasks such as setting routing tables, session tables, NAT tables, and firewall rules.
- **Example Analogy**: The cockpit and navigation systems of an airplane, where flight paths and rules are defined.
- **Location Example**: Management interface and configuration software of a switch.

## Controlling Trust

### Adaptive Identity
- **Concept**: Dynamically adjusts authentication strength based on context.
- **Process**: Considers factors such as the source, requested resources, physical location, connection type, IP address, and other metadata.
- **Example Action**: Increases authentication requirements (e.g., additional MFA prompts) based on risk indicators.

### Threat Scope Reduction
- **Concept**: Minimizes potential entry points to reduce the attack surface.
- **Action**: Limits access to specific resources like VPNs or physical locations.

### Policy-Driven Access Control
- **Concept**: Combines adaptive identity with predefined rules to decide appropriate authentication and access levels based on collected data.

## Security Zones

- **Concept**: Security involves understanding the overall path of communication, not just a one-to-one relationship between users and servers.
- **Definition**: Broad categorizations that provide a security foundation (e.g., Trusted/Untrusted, Internal/External, specific VPNs or departments).
- **Key Question**: "Where are you coming from and where are you going?"
- **Examples of Zones**:
  - Trusted vs. Untrusted
  - Internal network vs. External network
  - Specific VPNs (e.g., VPN 1, 5, 11)
  - Organizational departments (Marketing, IT, Accounting, Human Resources)

## Policy Enforcement Point (PEP) and Policy Decision Point (PDP)

### Policy Enforcement Point (PEP)
- **Role**: Acts as the "gatekeeper" for enforcing access decisions.
- **Location**: Between the subject/system and enterprise resources.
- **Subjects and Systems**: End users, applications, IoT devices.
- **Function**: Ensures all traffic passes through it before reaching its destination.

### Policy Decision Point (PDP)
- **Role**: The process that makes authentication decisions.
- **Components**:
  - **Policy Engine**: Evaluates access requests against predefined policies.
  - **Policy Administrator**: Communicates with the PEP and generates credentials based on the decision made by the Policy Engine.

## Zero Trust Across Planes

### Flow
1. **Subject/System** initiates a request from an Untrusted zone (Data Plane).
2. Request goes through the Policy Enforcement Point (PEP) (Data Plane).
3. PEP provides information to the Policy Decision Point (Control Plane).
4. PDP sends the request to the Policy Engine (Control Plane).
5. Policy Engine evaluates the request and makes a decision (grant/deny/revoke).
6. Decision is sent back to the Policy Administrator.
7. Policy Administrator generates credentials/tokens and instructs the PEP on whether to allow or disallow access.
8. If allowed, PEP provides access to an Enterprise Resource in a Trusted zone (Data Plane).

This model ensures that trust is never implicit but always explicitly verified at every step.

## Exam Takeaways

1. **Fundamental Principle**: Zero Trust operates on the principle of "never trust, always verify." No entity is inherently trusted.
2. **Holistic Approach**: Zero Trust covers everything—devices, processes, people, and connections, not just perimeter security.
3. **Key Problem Solved**: Traditional networks often have weak internal controls once breached by the firewall. Zero Trust addresses this with granular security throughout the network.
4. **Planes of Operation**:
   - **Data Plane**: Handles traffic flow and data processing.
   - **Control Plane**: Manages policies and rules, defining how data is processed.
5. **Controlling Trust Mechanisms**:
   - **Adaptive Identity**: Dynamically assesses risk based on multiple indicators to determine authentication strength.
   - **Threat Scope Reduction**: Minimizes potential entry points.
   - **Policy-Driven Access Control**: Combines adaptive identity with predefined rules.
6. **Security Zones**: Broad categorizations that define logical segments for applying access policies.
7. **Core Components**:
   - **PEP**: The "gatekeeper" enforcing access decisions.
   - **PDP**: The "brain" making authentication decisions, consisting of a Policy Engine and a Policy Administrator.

By understanding these key concepts, you can effectively implement Zero Trust security measures in various network scenarios.
