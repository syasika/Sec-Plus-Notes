# 1. Introduction to the CIA Triad

## Definition
The CIA Triad refers to a combination of three fundamental principles that form the core objectives of IT security. It is an easy way to remember the basics of information security.

## Alternative Name
Sometimes, it is referred to as the AIC Triad to differentiate it from the Central Intelligence Agency (US federal organization).

## Components (Security Objectives)
- **Confidentiality**
- **Integrity**
- **Availability**

---

## 2. Confidentiality

### Core Principle
Certain information should only be known to certain people.

### Objective
Prevent unauthorized disclosure of information to unauthorized individuals or systems.

### Analogy
Imagine a wallet secured with a chain and a combination lock, ensuring that only the authorized person can access it.

### Methods/Controls to Achieve Confidentiality

#### Encryption
- **Definition**: Encodes messages so only specific, authorized people can read them.
- **Process**: If encrypted data is intercepted, it appears as gibberish (ciphertext) to unauthorized parties.
- **Key Role**: Requires a key to decrypt back to original plaintext.

#### Access Controls
- **Definition**: Selectively restrict access to a resource.
- **Examples**: The marketing department can access marketing presentations but not accounting data.
- **Implementation**: Through permissions, roles, and groups.

#### Two-Factor Authentication (2FA)
- **Definition**: Requires additional confirmation beyond a password before information is disclosed.
- **Examples**: A code from a phone or biometric scan.
- **Process**: Adds an extra layer of security, making it harder for unauthorized users to gain access even if they have one credential.

---

## 3. Integrity

### Core Principle
Data should be stored and transferred as intended without unauthorized or accidental modification.

### Objective
Ensure that any modification to the data would be identified; data must remain accurate, complete, and trustworthy.

### Analogy
Think of a document with a "thumbs up" and a checkmark, indicating verification and approval.

### Methods/Controls to Achieve Integrity

#### Hashing
- **Definition**: A function that maps data of an arbitrary length to data of a fixed length (a hash value or digest).
- **Process**: Sender computes a hash of the data, sends both the data and the hash. Receiver computes a hash of the received data.
- **Verification**: If the receiver's hash matches the sender's hash, the data has not been modified in transit. Even a single bit change results in a different hash.

#### Digital Signatures
- **Definition**: A mathematical scheme to verify both the integrity of data and the authenticity of the sender.
- **Process**: Takes a hash of the data and encrypts it using the sender's private key (asymmetric encryption).
- **Verification**: Receiver uses the sender's public key to decrypt the signature, revealing the original hash. They then compute a hash of the received data. If both hashes match, the data is integral, and the sender’s identity is confirmed.

#### Certificates
- **Definition**: Used in conjunction with digital signatures to verify an individual or device.
- **Examples**: SSL/TLS certificates for websites provide additional factors of integrity.

#### Non-Repudiation
- **Definition**: A concept related to integrity, providing proof of both data integrity and origin.
- **Process**: Ensures that a sender cannot deny having sent a message, nor can a receiver deny having received it.
- **Achievement**: Through digital signatures, which cryptographically link the sender to the data.

---

## 4. Availability

### Core Principle
Information and systems must be accessible to authorized users when needed.

### Objective
Ensure that systems and networks are always up and running; data is always available.

### Analogy
Imagine a stack of hard drives, representing data storage that must always be online and accessible.

### Methods/Controls to Achieve Availability

#### Redundancy
- **Definition**: Building services with duplicate components or systems so they will always be available.
- **Examples**: Redundant power supplies, network links, servers, and data backups.

#### Fault Tolerance
- **Definition**: A system's ability to continue operating without interruption even when one or more of its components fail.
- **Examples**: RAID configurations for hard drives, load balancing, clustering.

#### Patching
- **Definition**: Regularly updating software, operating systems, and applications.
- **Benefits**: Improves system stability (fixes bugs) and closes security holes (vulnerabilities).
- **Outcome**: Prevents attackers from exploiting known weaknesses that could lead to system downtime or compromise, thereby maintaining availability.

## Takeaways for Exam Preparation

1. **Memorize the Acronym**: CIA (Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability) and its alternative, AIC.
2. **Define Each Principle Clearly**: Be able to articulate the core meaning and objective of Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability in your own words.
3. **Identify Key Controls/Mechanisms for Each**:
    - **Confidentiality**: Encryption, Access Controls, Two-Factor Authentication.
    - **Integrity**: Hashing, Digital Signatures, Certificates, Non-repudiation.
    - **Availability**: Redundancy, Fault Tolerance, Patching.
4. **Distinguish Between Similar Concepts**:
    - **Hashing vs. Digital Signatures**: Hashing verifies data integrity; digital signatures verify both data integrity and sender authenticity (and provide non-repudiation).
    - **Redundancy vs. Fault Tolerance**: Redundancy is having duplicates, while fault tolerance is the ability to continue operating despite failures, often achieved through redundancy.
5. **Understand Interrelationships/Potential Conflicts**:
    - **Confidentiality vs. Availability**: Extremely strong security (e.g., complex access controls, frequent key changes) could make data harder for authorized users to access, impacting availability.
    - **Integrity vs. Availability**: Strict integrity checks might slow down data processing, potentially affecting availability.
6. **Think of Real-World Examples**:
    - **Confidentiality**: Encrypted emails, password-protected documents, login with username/password + SMS code.
    - **Integrity**: Downloading a file and checking its MD5 hash, digitally signed software updates, blockchain technology.
    - **Availability**: Multiple web servers for popular websites, redundant power supplies in data centers, regular software updates for your phone/computer.
7. **Focus on "Why"**: For each concept and control, ask yourself why it is important and what problem it solves. This will help you understand, not just memorize.
