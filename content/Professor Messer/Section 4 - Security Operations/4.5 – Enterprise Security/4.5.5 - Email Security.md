# Email Security

## 1. Email Security Challenges

### Core Problem
- **Definition**: The original email transfer protocols, like SMTP, lack robust security features, making it easy for attackers to **spoof** emails.

### Spoofing
- **Definition**: An email appears to be from a legitimate sender (e.g., `james@professormesser.com`) but is actually sent by an unauthorized party.
- **Frequency**: This happens frequently—check your spam folder!
- **Impact**: It poses significant risks for individuals and organizations.

### Solution Need
- Additional security measures are required to verify the true origin of emails. These can be added to DNS servers as publicly available records.

## 2. Mail Gateway

### Function
- Acts as a "gatekeeper" for email traffic.

### Purpose
- Evaluates the legitimacy of incoming email messages before they reach your inbox.

### Action
- Blocks illegitimate emails (spam, spoofed mail) at the gateway *before* they reach you.

### Deployment
- **On-site**: Placed in a screened subnet (DMZ), between the internet and internal network, behind a firewall. It needs to communicate with the internet.
- **Cloud-based**: Many third-party services offer mail gateway functionality in the cloud, reducing the burden on internal infrastructure.

### Process
1. Email arrives from the internet at the Mail Gateway.
2. The Mail Gateway checks the email for validity (e.g., sender authentication).
3. If valid, it passes through to the Internal Mail Server and then to your inbox.
4. If not valid, it is discarded or sent to spam/junk folder.

## 3. Sender Policy Framework (SPF)

### Protocol
- Used by senders to explicitly define which mail servers are authorized to send email for their domain.

### Mechanism
- Senders configure a list of authorized mail servers in a DNS TXT record, which is publicly available on the sender's DNS server.

### How It Works
1. A receiving mail server (often via its mail gateway) receives an email.
2. It queries the DNS server of the *sending domain* to retrieve the SPF TXT record.
3. It checks if the *IP address of the mail server that sent the email* is listed as authorized in the SPF record.
4. If the sending IP is not on the list, the email is flagged as potentially spoofed or illegitimate.

### Example SPF TXT Record
```
professormesser.com. 300 IN TXT "v=spf1 include:mailgun.org ~all"
- `v=spf1`: Specifies the SPF version.
- `include:mailgun.org`: Authorizes mail servers listed in Mailgun's SPF record to send email for `professormesser.com`.
- `~all`: **Softfail**. Indicates that emails from servers not explicitly listed should be treated with suspicion (e.g., marked as spam), but not necessarily rejected outright.
```

## 4. Domain Keys Identified Mail (DKIM)

### Protocol
- Provides a way to digitally sign outgoing email messages.

### Purpose
- Verifies the authenticity of the sender (that the email truly came from the claimed domain).
- Ensures the integrity of the email content (that it hasn't been tampered with in transit).

### Mechanism
- A mail server signs all outgoing mail using a private key. The corresponding public key is published in a DNS TXT record for the sending domain.

### How It Works
1. The sending mail server signs the outgoing email with its private key.
2. The receiving mail server receives the email.
3. It queries the sender's DNS server to retrieve the DKIM public key (from a specific DKIM TXT record, often with a selector in the hostname, e.g., `selector._domainkey.professormesser.com`).
4. It uses this public key to validate the digital signature on the email.
5. If the signature validates, it confirms the email's origin and integrity.

### Visibility
- The DKIM signature is validated by receiving mail servers but is not usually seen by end-users (it’s in the email headers).

### Key Difference from SPF
- **SPF** checks *which servers are allowed to send email for a domain*.
- **DKIM** checks *if the email itself was sent by an authorized server and hasn't been altered*.

## 5. Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance (DMARC)

### Protocol
- An extension of SPF and DKIM that tells receiving mail servers what to do with emails that fail SPF or DKIM validation, and provides reporting on email authentication results.

### Purpose
- Provides a policy for handling emails that do not validate with SPF and DKIM.
- Offers reporting mechanisms for domain owners to see how their emails are being received and authenticated across the internet.

### Mechanism
- The domain owner defines a policy in a DNS TXT record.

### Policy Options (defined in the `p` tag)
- `p=none`: **Monitor only.** Take no specific action; simply report on non-compliant emails.
- `p=quarantine`: Send to spam.
- `p=reject`: Block.

### Reporting
- **rua**: Specifies an email address to receive compliance reports, helping domain owners monitor email authentication status.

## Takeaways for Exam Preparation

1. **Understand the Problem**: The fundamental issue is that native email protocols lack robust sender authentication, leading to easy email spoofing.
2. **Know the Three Protocols (SPF, DKIM, DMARC) and Their Relationship**:
    - **SPF**: *Who* is allowed to send email for my domain (based on IP address). Prevents direct spoofing.
    - **DKIM**: *Is the email signed by an authorized server for my domain, and has it been tampered with?* (Uses digital signatures with public/private keys). Prevents content alteration and verifies sender.
    - **DMARC**: *What should receiving servers do if SPF or DKIM fail, and tell me about it?* (Policy and reporting). Provides a comprehensive framework built on top of SPF and DKIM.
3. **All Three Use DNS TXT Records**: This is a crucial implementation detail.
4. **Mail Gateway Role**: It's the first line of defense, evaluating incoming emails and enforcing policies (including SPF, DKIM, DMARC checks) before they reach the internal mail server or user inbox.
5. **Key Terms & Their Meanings**:
    - **Spoofing**: Impersonating a sender.
    - **Mail Gateway**: The "gatekeeper" for inbound email.
    - **SPF TXT record**: Lists authorized sending IPs.
    - `~all` (SPF): Softfail (suspicious).
    - `-all` (SPF): Hardfail (reject).
    - **DKIM TXT record**: Contains the public key for digital signatures.
    - **DMARC TXT record**: Defines policy for failed authentication.
    - `p=none` (DMARC): Monitor.
    - `p=quarantine` (DMARC): Send to spam.
    - `p=reject` (DMARC): Block.
    - `rua` (DMARC): Reporting address.

## Potential Exam Questions

1. **Question**: Explain why email spoofing is a common problem and what fundamental security weakness in email protocols contributes to it.
   - **Answer Focus**: Lack of inherent sender authentication/verification in core SMTP.

2. **Question**: Describe the role of a mail gateway in an email security architecture. Where is it typically deployed, and why?
   - **Answer Focus**: Gatekeeper, evaluates inbound, blocks before user, screened subnet/DMZ or cloud.

3. **Question**: Differentiate between SPF, DKIM, and DMARC protocols in terms of their primary function and how they enhance email security.
   - **Answer Focus**: SPF = authorized senders (IPs), DKIM = digital signature/integrity, DMARC = policy for failures + reporting.

4. **Question**: A domain administrator wants to implement SPF for their domain. What type of DNS record would they configure, and what would a `~all` directive in their SPF record signify?
   - **Answer Focus**: DNS TXT record, `~all` = softfail, treat as suspicious.

5. **Question**: How does DKIM help verify the integrity of an email message? What cryptographic concept is central to its operation?
   - **Answer Focus**: Digital signatures, public/private key cryptography.

6. **Question**: A company has implemented SPF and DKIM, but is still concerned about spoofed emails reaching user inboxes. They want to ensure that emails failing authentication are rejected outright. Which protocol should they implement or modify, and what specific policy setting would achieve this?
   - **Answer Focus**: DMARC, `p=reject`.

7. **Question**: What is the purpose of the `rua` tag in a DMARC record?
   - **Answer Focus**: Specifies an email address to receive compliance reports, helping domain owners monitor email authentication status.

8. **Question**: An email arrives claiming to be from your bank. Your mail gateway performs an SPF check and finds that the sending IP is not authorized by the bank's SPF record. What action might a properly configured DMARC policy for the bank's domain tell your mail gateway to take? List the possible actions.
   - **Answer Focus**: `p=none` (monitor), `p=quarantine` (spam), `p=reject` (block).
