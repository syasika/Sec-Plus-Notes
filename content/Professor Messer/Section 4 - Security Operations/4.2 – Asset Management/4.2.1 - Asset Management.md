# Asset Management

## 1. Acquisition/Procurement Process
### Definition
The acquisition or procurement process is a formal, multi-step procedure used by organizations to request and obtain goods and services from third-party vendors.

### Formal Process
This process is often highly structured, requiring sign-offs and approvals from various departments within the organization (e.g., IT, purchasing, management). This ensures accountability and adherence to budgetary constraints.

### Key Steps

1. **Request from the User**
   - Initiated by an end-user or department that identifies a need for specific hardware, software, or services.
   - Includes detailed budgeting information to ensure funds are available.
   - Requires formal approvals from relevant stakeholders and management before proceeding.

2. **Negotiate with Suppliers**
   - Involves communication and bargaining between the acquiring organization and potential suppliers.
   - Covers critical aspects such as pricing, specific terms and conditions (e.g., delivery, support), licensing agreements (for software), and overall contract details.
   - The goal is to secure the best possible value and terms for the organization.

3. **Purchase, Invoice, and Payment**
   - Once negotiations are finalized and a contract is agreed upon, the actual purchase is made.
   - The supplier provides the agreed-upon goods or services.
   - An invoice detailing the cost is then issued by the supplier.
   - Payment terms are pre-negotiated and dictate when the invoice must be paid in full (e.g., immediately upon receipt, Net 30 days, Net 60 days).

## 2. Assignment/Accounting
### Central Asset Tracking System
A critical system used across different parts of the organization to manage, monitor, and track all assets throughout their entire lifecycle, from acquisition to disposal.

### Ownership
- The process of associating a specific individual or department with an asset.
- Example: A laptop is assigned to a particular user.
- **Usefulness:** Crucial for accountability, knowing who to contact for maintenance or updates, and tracking the asset's location and status.

### Classification
Assets are categorized based on their type, which has significant implications for accounting and taxation:
- **Hardware:** Typically classified as a **capital expenditure (CapEx)**.
  - Hardware assets usually have a useful life of more than one year.
  - They are subject to depreciation over time, which reduces their book value and affects the organization's tax liabilities.
- **Software:** Generally classified as an **operating expenditure (OpEx)**.
  - Software typically does not depreciate in the same way hardware does.
  - It is taxed differently, often expensed in the year it’s acquired or licensed.

## 3. Monitoring / Asset Tracking
### Inventory Every Asset
Maintaining a comprehensive and up-to-date list of all organizational assets is fundamental:
- Examples: Laptops, desktops, servers, routers, switches, cables, fiber modules, tablets, etc.
- Ensures all assets are accounted for and their status is known.

### Associate a Support Ticket with a Device Make and Model
When a user reports an issue, linking the support ticket directly to the specific device's make and model in the asset tracking system:
- **Benefit:** Provides technicians with more detailed and accurate information than a user's description, streamlining troubleshooting and repair processes.

### Enumeration
The process of listing all individual parts or components that make up a larger asset. For example, for a desktop computer, enumeration would include the CPU, memory modules, storage drives, keyboard, mouse, and any other peripherals:
- **Benefit:** Allows the organization to track not just the main asset but also its internal components, which is useful for upgrades, repairs, and understanding system configurations.

### Add an Asset Tag
Physical labels or tags attached directly to devices for easy identification. Can include barcodes, RFID tags, visible tracking numbers, or the organization's name/logo:
- **Functionality:** Aids in physical inventory, quick identification, and serves as a security feature (deterring theft, helping recover lost/stolen items).

## 4. Media Sanitization
### System Disposal or Decommissioning
The process of thoroughly removing data from storage media to ensure that no usable or sensitive information remains:
- Different use cases: 
  - Clean a hard drive for future use (internal reuse): Data is securely erased, but the drive remains functional for another user within the organization.
  - Permanently delete a single file: Ensuring specific sensitive files are unrecoverable, even if the rest of the drive is still in use.

### A One-Way Trip
The objective of secure sanitization is that once data is removed, it is truly gone and cannot be recovered, even by advanced forensic tools:
- **Reuse the Storage Media:** Proper sanitization allows organizations to reuse or reallocate storage devices without the risk of sensitive data from previous users being accessed by others. This is a cost-effective and secure practice.

## 5. Physical Destruction
### Certificate of Destruction
For large volumes of storage devices, organizations may outsource data destruction to specialized third-party vendors:
- **Confirmation Needed:** When using a third party, it's essential for the organization to receive official confirmation that their data has been securely and completely destroyed.
- **Service Should Include a Certificate:** A reputable destruction service will provide a **Certificate of Destruction**. This formal document details which drives were destroyed, the method used, and often includes serial numbers for verification.

### Third-Party Destruction
The certificate serves as a critical paper trail, providing documented proof of data destruction:
- Vital for regulatory compliance, internal auditing, and legal accountability, ensuring the organization knows exactly what happened to its sensitive data.

## 6. Data Retention

### Backup Your Data
The practice of systematically saving copies of data to ensure its availability in case of loss or corruption:
- **Considerations:** How much data to back up, where the backups are stored (on-site, off-site, cloud), the number of copies, versions of copies, the overall lifecycle of the data, and policies for purging old, irrelevant data.

### Regulatory Compliance
Many industries and jurisdictions have laws and regulations that mandate the retention of certain types of data for specific periods:
- Examples: Financial institutions must retain corporate financial data for several years; healthcare organizations have rules for patient records; some legal requirements dictate email retention.
- Organizations must establish clear policies and procedures to meet these mandatory data retention requirements.

### Operational Needs
Data retention also serves practical business needs beyond compliance:
- **Accidental deletion:** Allows for recovery of data that was unintentionally deleted by users or system errors.
- **Disaster recovery:** Enables the restoration of operations and data after catastrophic events (e.g., natural disaster, major cyberattack) by retrieving backups from another location.

### Differentiate by Type and Application
Not all data requires the same retention period or backup strategy:
- Organizations need to categorize data by type (e.g., customer data, intellectual property, internal memos, transactional data) and application.
- **Goal:** To establish policies that ensure the right data is retained for the appropriate length of time, allowing for efficient recovery of specific information when and where it is needed.

## Takeaways for Exam

1. **Acquisition & Procurement (The "Buying" Process):** Understand it's a formal, multi-step process involving user requests, budgeting, negotiation with suppliers (terms, pricing, licensing), and then the actual purchase, invoicing, and payment. Approvals are key.
2. **Asset Tracking (The "Managing" Process):** A central system is crucial for the entire asset lifecycle. Key elements include assigning ownership to individuals, classifying assets (hardware as capital expenditure, software as operating expenditure – remember the tax implications!), and maintaining detailed inventory.
3. **Monitoring & Detailed Tracking (The "Knowing" Process):** Beyond basic inventory, actively monitor assets. This includes associating support tickets with device make/model for better troubleshooting, enumerating internal components (CPU, memory, etc.), and using asset tags (barcodes, RFID) for physical identification and security.
4. **Media Sanitization (The "Cleaning" Process):** Essential for system disposal, decommissioning, or reuse. The core principle is to completely remove data so that "no usable information remains" – it's a "one-way trip" with no forensic recovery.
5. **Physical Destruction (The "Guaranteed Gone" Process):** When data must be absolutely unrecoverable, physical destruction is used. Know the various methods: shredding/pulverizing, drilling/hammering (destroying platters), degaussing (magnetic field removal for HDDs), and incineration.
6. **Certificate of Destruction (The "Proof" Process):** When using third-party services for data destruction, always obtain a Certificate of Destruction. This document provides a critical paper trail for compliance, auditing, and legal proof that data has been destroyed.
7. **Data Retention (The "Keeping" Process):** Just as important as destruction. Understand why data is retained: for backups (copies, versions, lifecycle management), regulatory compliance (mandated retention of emails, financial data), and operational needs (accidental deletion recovery, disaster recovery). Retention policies should differentiate by data type and application.
